## 1. Fogg Behavior Model
**Evidence** B as a widely used design model (Fogg 2009, 2019). It has limited formal testing as a predictive model.

**What it says.** A behavior happens when motivation, ability and a prompt converge. When motivation is low, the behavior must be very easy. Prompts only work when the user is above the action line.

**Apply.**
- When users don't act, first make the action easier (fewer steps, less time, less thinking, less money, fewer social or routine conflicts) before trying to raise motivation.
- Time prompts to moments of high motivation (right after a success, when a need arises).
- Make the prompt the action itself where possible (a button in the notification that completes the task).

**Audit question.** For the key action, is the problem motivation, ability or a missing prompt?

## 2. Time to value and the "aha" moment
**Evidence** C (industry practice. Activation metrics correlate with retention in many product analytics datasets, though causality is often assumed).

**What it says.** Users decide early whether a product is worth it. The sooner they experience the core value, the more likely they stay.

**Apply.**
- Identify the action that predicts retention (first project created, first file shared, first playlist played) and design onboarding to reach it fast.
- Defer configuration, profile completion and invites until after first value.
- Use templates, sample data and smart defaults so the first experience isn't an empty screen.
- Let people try before signing up where possible.

**Audit question.** How many steps and minutes stand between landing and the first moment of real value?

## 3. Goal-gradient effect
**Evidence** A to B (Hull 1932 with rats. Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng 2006 found coffee card customers bought more often as they got closer to a free coffee, and web users rated more songs as they approached a reward).

**What it says.** Effort accelerates as a goal gets closer.

**Apply.**
- Show progress toward meaningful goals (profile completeness, loyalty rewards, course completion, free shipping threshold).
- Break long goals into nearer sub-goals so users are always close to the next milestone.
- Celebrate milestones to reset the gradient.

**Limits.** Progress bars toward meaningless goals (100% profile completion that serves only the company) feel manipulative.

**Audit question.** Can users see how close they are to the next meaningful milestone?

## 4. Endowed progress effect
**Evidence** B (Nunes and Drèze 2006. In a car wash field experiment, 34% of customers given a 10-stamp card with 2 stamps already filled redeemed it, against 19% given a blank 8-stamp card, despite needing the same 8 purchases).

**What it says.** Framing a task as already started increases persistence.

**Apply.**
- Count steps already completed in onboarding checklists ("Account created" ticked first).
- Show completed steps in multi-step flows.
- Give a genuine head start on loyalty or rewards.

**Limits.** The head start should reflect real progress or a real gift. Fake progress is easy to see through.

**Audit question.** Does the user see the progress they've already made?

## 5. Progress indicators
**Evidence** B. In survey research, Conrad et al. 2010 and the Villar, Callegaro and Yang 2013 meta-analysis found constant-speed progress bars don't reliably reduce dropout. Bars that start slow increase dropout. Bars that start fast and slow down can reduce it. Harrison et al. 2007 and 2010 found progress bars with accelerating behavior and certain animations feel faster.

**Apply.**
- Show step indicators in multi-step flows with step names ("Delivery, Payment, Review"), so users know what's coming.
- Make early steps quick and easy so progress feels fast at the start.
- For loading, use determinate progress when duration is known. Front-load visible progress where truthful.
- Don't show "Step 1 of 12" when the flow is long. Group into a few named stages.

**Audit question.** Does the user know where they are, how much is left, and does progress feel fast at the start?

## 6. Resumption (Ovsiankina effect)
**Evidence** B (Ghibellini and Meier 2025 meta-analysis confirmed a general tendency to resume interrupted tasks, while the memory advantage claimed by the Zeigarnik effect did not replicate).

**Apply.**
- Make resuming effortless (autosave, deep links back to the exact step, "Continue setup" on the home screen).
- An open checklist with a few items left invites completion.

**Audit question.** Is there always an obvious way back into an unfinished task?

## 7. IKEA effect and effort justification
**Evidence** B (Norton, Mochon and Ariely 2012. People valued things they assembled more highly, but only when they completed them).

**Apply.**
- Let users build and customize meaningful things early (their workspace, their plan, their profile).
- Keep the effort achievable. Failure or incompletion removes the effect.

**Limits.** Don't add effort for its own sake. Unnecessary work is friction.

**Audit question.** Do users invest a little effort that produces something they feel is theirs?

## 8. Self-determination theory
**Evidence** A as a motivation theory (Ryan and Deci 2000, extensive research). B for application to product design (Peters, Calvo and Ryan 2018).

**What it says.** Lasting, high-quality motivation comes from autonomy (I chose this), competence (I'm good at this) and relatedness (I'm connected to others).

**Apply.**
- **Autonomy.** Offer meaningful choices, avoid coercive language, let users skip and customize, explain rather than command.
- **Competence.** Clear feedback, achievable challenges, visible mastery, helpful errors that teach.
- **Relatedness.** Collaboration, community, human tone, recognition from peers.

**Audit question.** Does this flow make the user feel in control, capable and connected, or pushed, confused and alone?

## 9. Flow
**Evidence** B (Csikszentmihalyi 1990. Applied widely in games and HCI).

**What it says.** People reach deep, enjoyable focus when goals are clear, feedback is immediate, and challenge matches skill.

**Apply.**
- Remove interruptions during focused tasks (no modals or upsells mid-task).
- Instant feedback on actions.
- Adapt difficulty, offer shortcuts for experts and guidance for novices.

**Audit question.** Does anything break concentration during the core task?

## 10. Small wins and the progress principle
**Evidence** B (Amabile and Kramer 2011, diary study of knowledge workers showing progress in meaningful work was the strongest daily motivator).

**Apply.**
- Make progress visible (streaks of real activity, completed items, outcomes achieved).
- Acknowledge small successes with light feedback.
- Weekly summaries of what the user accomplished.

**Audit question.** Does the product show users the progress they're making?

## 11. Implementation intentions
**Evidence** A (Gollwitzer and Sheeran 2006 meta-analysis, d = 0.65 for goal attainment).

**What it says.** "When X happens, I will do Y" plans dramatically raise follow-through.

**Apply.**
- Ask users when and where they'll do the activity ("When do you want to practice? Morning, lunch, evening") and remind them then.
- Calendar integration for planned actions.
- Let users pick their own reminder time.

**Audit question.** Do we help users plan when they'll act, or just hope they return?

## 12. Fresh start effect
**Evidence** B (Dai, Milkman and Riis 2014. Goal-related behavior rises after temporal landmarks such as new weeks, months, birthdays and new years).

**Apply.**
- Time goal-setting prompts and re-engagement campaigns to landmarks (Monday, the 1st of the month, New Year, the user's anniversary with the product).
- Offer "start fresh" options after lapses instead of showing a broken streak.

**Audit question.** Are re-engagement moments aligned with natural fresh starts?

## 13. Habit formation
**Evidence** B (Lally et al. 2010. Time to automaticity had a median of 66 days with a range of 18 to 254. Missing a single day didn't derail habit formation).

**Apply.**
- Anchor the product to an existing routine (after morning coffee, at the end of the workday).
- Keep the repeated action small and consistent.
- Forgive missed days. Streak freezes or "repair" options keep motivation alive. Breaking a streak shouldn't feel like losing everything.

**Audit question.** Is the core repeated action small, cued by an existing routine, and forgiving of lapses?

## 14. Variable rewards and the Hook model
**Evidence** C for the Hook model as a design framework (Eyal 2014). A for variable reinforcement in animal learning. Strong ethical concerns, and regulators in the EU are targeting addictive design.

**What it says.** Trigger, action, variable reward and investment can create habitual use. Unpredictable rewards are especially compelling.

**Apply ethically.**
- Variety in genuinely valuable content (new recommendations, fresh discoveries) is fine.
- Ask "would the user endorse this habit on reflection?" If the answer is no, don't build it.
- Avoid infinite scroll, autoplay and slot-machine mechanics in products where compulsive use harms users, and never for children.

**Audit question.** If users understood exactly why they keep coming back, would they be glad?

## 15. Gamification and the overjustification effect
**Evidence** B. Meta-analyses (Sailer and Homner 2020) find gamification has small to medium positive effects on learning, depending on design. Deci, Koestner and Ryan 1999 meta-analysis found expected tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks.

**Apply.**
- Use points, badges and leaderboards to make progress and competence visible. Avoid using them as bribes for the activity itself.
- Prefer informational feedback ("You've mastered fractions") over controlling rewards.
- Leaderboards demotivate those far from the top. Use small cohorts, friends, or personal bests.

**Audit question.** Do game elements support the activity's real purpose, or replace it?

## 16. Personalized onboarding
**Evidence** C (industry case studies. Consistent with SDT and relevance research).

**Apply.**
- Ask one to three questions about goals or role, then tailor the first experience.
- Show immediately how the answer changed things ("Here's your sales template").
- Every question must change the experience. Drop any that don't.

**Audit question.** Does every onboarding question visibly improve what the user gets?

## 17. Prompts, notifications and permission timing
**Evidence** B (industry data consistently shows contextual permission requests outperform requests on first launch. Mark, Gudith and Klocke 2008 found interruptions raise stress).

**Apply.**
- Ask for permissions (notifications, location, camera, contacts) at the moment they're needed, after explaining the benefit with an in-app pre-permission screen. On iOS, a denied system prompt is hard to reverse.
- Notifications should be timely, personal and actionable. Let users choose types and frequency.
- Batch low-urgency notifications into digests.
- Track opt-outs and uninstalls as guardrail metrics.

**Audit question.** Is every permission requested in context with a clear benefit, and is every notification something the user would want?

## 18. Reactance
**Evidence** A (Brehm 1966. Robust across contexts).

**What it says.** When people feel their freedom is threatened, they push back and may do the opposite.

**Apply.**
- Avoid coercive language ("You must", "Don't miss out", all-caps warnings).
- Offer choices and a clear way to say no.
- Explain the reason behind requirements.
- Don't block content behind aggressive gates on first visit.

**Audit question.** Does any message make the user feel cornered?
